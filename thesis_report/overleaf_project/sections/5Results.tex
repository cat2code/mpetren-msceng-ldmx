\subsubsection{Detector Occupancy and Longitudinal Shower Development}

Figure~\ref{fig:results_shower_xy_context} shows the pooled transverse
distribution of reconstructed \gls{ecal} hits, in detector coordinates. The hits are pooled together along the beam direction flattened into a two-dimensional image. Hits are registered over the entire field, but the
highest occupancy and energy density occur near the beam region (which can be reminded is $8\text{cm} \times 2 \text{cm}$) located at the center. The width of the highest density seems to be wider than $2\text{cm}$ which is explained by the vertical magnetic field inferred by the dipole magnet that distorts the beam in the horizontal direction due to Lenz's law. The energy density is also comparatively more gravitated to the center compared to the transverse distribution of hits. The energy is weighted to the center because the first hits are more energetic and located near the beam region. As the shower develops through the layers it spreads in the transverse direction but this also corresponds to loss of energy. The discrete pattern of populated bins follows the hexagonal cell geometry and should not be interpreted as a continuous image. On average, the selected \(2e\) events contain \(247.2\) reconstructed hits and \(16.01\,\mathrm{GeV}\) of
reconstructed energy, compared with \(354.6\) hits and
\(24.02\,\mathrm{GeV}\) for \(3e\).

Simulation-flagged noise hits are rare in the production sample. Across the
one-million-event mixed-multiplicity sample used for the final contributor
model, \(27{,}603\) of \(300{,}828{,}260\) retained \gls{ecal} hits carry the
noise flag. This corresponds to \(0.0092\,\%\) of the hits, or \(0.0276\)
flagged noise hits per event on average (approximately one per 36 events).
The latter is a hit-level rate and should not be interpreted as the fraction
of events containing noise. The baseline models and the descriptive shower
figures remove these hits, whereas the contributor model retains them as a
background class for prediction.

\subsubsection{Truth-Level Transverse Shower Geometry}

The truth-assisted geometry (that we can know only because the data is simulated) is defined in
Equations~\eqref{eq:shower_geometry_weight}--\eqref{eq:min_shower_separation}.
It weights each hit by reconstructed energy and the simulated contribution
fraction, so a mixed hit can contribute to several shower geometries. These
quantities are unavailable in measured detector data and are used only to
characterise the sample and interpret model performance.

Figure~\ref{fig:results_truth_shower_geometry} combines the closest pair of
resulting shower centroids in each event with the corresponding truth-shower
width distributions. The left column resolves separation along the two
transverse coordinates and then normalises the two-dimensional centroid
distance by the combined radial shower width. The right column shows the
individual shower widths in \(x\), \(y\) and radius.
The effective Moli\`ere radius \(R_{\mathrm{M}}=25\,\mathrm{mm}\) provides a
reference scale. For \(2e\) events, the median minimum separation is
\(0.253R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(6.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)) in \(x\) and
\(0.946R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(23.7\,\mathrm{mm}\)) in \(y\). The corresponding
\(3e\) medians are \(0.093R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(2.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)) and
\(0.334R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(8.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)). The difference between the
\(x\) and \(y\)
distributions is a property of the generated sample rather than a universal
detector-resolution limit. Moreover, a \(3e\) event contains three distinct
combinations of electron pairs instead of one. Taking the closest pair therefore shifts the
minimum-separation distribution towards smaller values even if the
distribution for a single pair were unchanged.

The width panels separate the scale of an individual shower from the distance
between showers. The median transverse
widths are independent of event multiplicity: for both samples,
\(\sigma_x\simeq0.67R_{\mathrm{M}}\),
\(\sigma_y\simeq0.65R_{\mathrm{M}}\) and
\(\sigma_r\simeq0.96R_{\mathrm{M}}\). In contrast, the median minimum
width-normalised separation from
Equation~\eqref{eq:normalised_shower_separation} decreases from \(0.724\) for
\(2e\) to \(0.353\) for \(3e\). The individual
showers therefore have similar transverse dimensions in the two samples, but
the showers occupy a substantially more overlapping regime
when a third electron is present. This is the main physical explanation for
expecting the \(3e\) hit-assignment task to be more difficult, as more overlap implies more non-reversible ambiguity that complicates inference, yielding worse predictions.

The \gls{tpad} tracks provide a small set of online reconstructed upstream position and
photoelectron measurements in addition to the offline reconstructed \gls{ecal} hits. Their token multiplicity is already an informative  electron-count proxy, but it is not a complete one. Figure~\ref{fig:results_tpad_multiplicity} shows that the
observed multiplicity equals the generated count in \(85.7\,\%\) of \(2e\)
events and \(74.0\,\%\) of \(3e\) events. Most of the remaining \(2e\) events
contain one token (\(13.9\,\%\)), while \(23.7\,\%\) of the \(3e\) events
contain only two. A further \(2.2\,\%\) of the \(3e\) sample contains only one
token, whereas overcounting is negligible for both multiplicities, which is an intended result of the design and implementation of the \gls{tpad}. Across the
balanced \(2e/3e\) sample, direct equality gives \(79.9\,\%\)
electron-count accuracy. The multiplicity therefore carries useful global
information, but missing, merged or otherwise unresolved tracks prevent it
from determining the electron count by itself. This motivates testing whether
a model can combine the \gls{tpad} observations with the \gls{ecal} shower
pattern, at least in offline reconstruction it is highly expected that a reconstruction model can improve the electron count.

\subsubsection{Summarizing discussion}

In summary, the event statistics establish the expectations used to
interpret the model results. The individual shower have the same characteristics in
\(2e\) and \(3e\) events, but the closest-pair separation decreases
substantially for \(3e\) and showers have a higher degree of overlap, and this should be the main cause of increased difficulty in separating the electro showers in reconstruction. The \gls{tpad} multiplicity supplies useful but incomplete event-level context.

\subsubsection{Baseline Architecture and Performance Comparison}

The headline baseline comparison uses \(1{,}000{,}000\) selected events for
each electron multiplicity: \(800{,}000\) training, \(100{,}000\) validation
and \(100{,}000\) test events.
Both architectures receive the same log-transformed ECal and \gls{tpad}-track
features and use a hidden width of 128. The Transformer contains three
pre-layer-normalised self-attention layers with four attention heads and a
final layer normalisation. The GravNet model contains four neighbourhood
aggregation blocks, each using \(k=16\) neighbours, with batch normalisation
between blocks. Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_architecture} summarises the
resulting model sizes and observed training times.

\input{config/tables/results_baseline_architecture}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:method_capacity_selection}, these models were
selected as performance-oriented reference configurations rather than through
an exhaustive capacity search. The comparison below therefore evaluates the
implemented architectures, not necessarily an optimal accuracy-parameter or
accuracy-latency frontier. Simply put, these hyperparameter choices were the best found for the scope of this thesis, and one extra block was given to the GravNet model since it has a lower parameter count per layer compared to the transformer. In particular, the use of contemporary \gls{mlpf}
models as a qualitative scale reference does not imply matched capacity: the
CMS particle-flow problem has a substantially larger size of input, different inputs, outputs and reconstruction scope. No pruning or other sophisticated compression study was performed.


The GravNet baseline has approximately \(36\,\%\) fewer trainable parameters
and correspondingly smaller model weights and checkpoints. In these runs,
however, this did not translate into shorter epochs: Transformer optimisation
took approximately 38--40 minutes per epoch, compared with 47--50 minutes for
GravNet both training on NVIDIA A100 \gls{gpu}s. The learned neighbour search and aggregation are more expensive than
the parameter count alone suggests. This timing comparison remains
descriptive rather than controlled because the Transformer used a batch size
of 64 and GravNet used 32, though separate testing not presented here has not indicated that increasing the batch size of the GravNet to 64 would change this fact.

Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_performance} compares the checkpoints selected
by minimum validation loss on the held-out test sets. All assignment metrics
use one optimal event-wide
permutation of the predicted electron labels. Both architectures reach similar
performance, while the \(3e\) task is consistently more difficult than the
\(2e\) task. Energy weighting increases every accuracy, showing that the
misassigned hits carry less reconstructed energy on average than the correctly
assigned hits.

Next up metrics that are rather unique for \gls{ml}-based models, compared to rule-based ones, shall be investigated and it will be explored how these could potentially be exploited, if one desires to artificially boost accuracy or if one is OK with omitting some hits. Confidence and entropy are based on the logit outputs of the baseline models. The logit outputs are bounded between $p \, \in [0,1]$ and they provide a metric that could be interpreted as a probability, though these two does not have a perfect correspondence and they should not be confused with each other. \textit{Confidence} in Figure~\ref{fig:results_confidence} is defined as the mean max-softmax confidence, i.e the largest logit value in the vector that holds the logits corresponding to each electron label, and then calculating the mean over the whole event. The plot shows that high mean hit confidence is
associated with high event accuracy, while
Figure~\ref{fig:results_entropy} shows the complementary negative relation
between normalised prediction entropy and accuracy. Entropy is perhaps the more interesting metric of ''confidence'' as it captures the spread of logits of the different labels. Both summaries can
therefore rank difficult events, although neither relation by itself
establishes probability calibration. One also has to be somewhat careful with these metrics as they compare accuracy against a model derived metric, a metric only indirectly based on the actual properties of the event. This shown correlation is perhaps not surprising, if this pattern was not apparent the models would not be able to make successful predictions these go hand in hand, but these metrics could be interesting to explore deeper in how they could be exploited if a practical application arises.

\subsubsection{Trigger-Pad Ablation}

A paired inference-time ablation was performed with the best checkpoint of the
final \(3e\) pre-layer-normalised Transformer. The intact model and the same
model with every \gls{tpad}-track token removed were evaluated on the
identical \(100{,}000\) held-out test events. Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_tpad_ablation}
reports permutation-aligned results, consistently with the other baseline
hit-assignment metrics.

\input{config/tables/results_baseline_tpad_ablation}

Accuracy with the intact input exceeds the ablated evaluation by \(0.119\)
percentage points for pooled hit accuracy, \(0.125\) points for mean event
accuracy and \(0.243\) points for energy-weighted accuracy. All three paired
bootstrap intervals exclude zero, showing that the measured differences are
statistically resolved for this fixed checkpoint and test sample. Their
magnitude is nevertheless small: the largest difference is \(0.243\)
percentage points, and the median event-level accuracy change is zero.

The ablation therefore demonstrates sensitivity to removing the \gls{tpad}
tokens, but it cannot establish that useful detector information contained in
those tokens caused the difference. Removing an entire modality only at
inference time changes the model's input distribution and attention context.
A controlled comparison between separately trained ECal-only and
ECal-plus-\gls{tpad} models, preferably across several random seeds, would be
required to isolate the added value of \gls{tpad} information. The confidence
intervals quantify event-sampling uncertainty for this fixed checkpoint; they
do not include variation from retraining with different random seeds or
samples. The result is also specific to a \(3e\) task whose electron count is
supplied in advance.


\subsection{MLPF-Inspired Multi-Task Slot Model}

\subsubsection{Multi-Task Reconstruction Performance}

The experimental contributor-set slot model extends the fixed-multiplicity
assignment problem to a particle-flow-like multi-task setting. It was trained
on \(500{,}000\) \(2e\) and \(500{,}000\) \(3e\) events. The combined sample
was divided into \(800{,}000\) training, \(100{,}000\) validation and
\(100{,}000\) test events. With canonical ordering, $2e$ has roughly an equal distribution of \gls{ecal} hits with labels 1 and $3e$ events has also roughly and equal distribution of hits labels 1, 2 and 3. This means that the dataset has a lower number of \gls{ecal} hits with the label 3 and it means that all the hits with that specific label comes from a $3e$ event, while the hits with label 1 and 2 is mixed from both $2e$ and $3e$ events. Results use the epoch-15 checkpoint selected by
minimum validation loss. The
backbone has hidden width 192, three pre-layer-normalised Transformer layers
and eight attention heads (increased from 4 in the baseline model), corresponding to approximately \(1.16\) million
trainable parameters. The test set contains \(30{,}079{,}515\) ECal hits.

The model outputs logits in pre-determined vector sizes, and loss is based on these float values and the reconstructed metrics are based on a post-processing assessment based on these, thus yielding a ''slot based design'' inspired by the \gls{mlpf} model. The model predicts three electron-slot validity scores for each event and a
contributor-set distribution and energy fractions for each hit. The electron
count is derived from the valid slots (note that it is not produced by a separate count head as it was with the baseline model). Similarly, the dominant-origin label is derived from the predicted fractions. This post-processing keeps the reported quantities consistent with the model's underlying set-valued prediction. Table~\ref{tab:results_contributor_performance} summarises the native multi-task outputs.

\input{config/tables/results_contributor_performance}

Figure~\ref{fig:results_contributor_set_confusion} resolves the exact
contributor-set task. Pure one-electron contributions dominate the data and
are generally reconstructed more reliably than mixed sets, but among them the hits with label 2 has the lowest accuracy due to the same effect discussed in \ref{sec:results_detailed_hit_assignment} and about 1/3 of these hits should come from a $3e$ event. The overall exact
set accuracy is \(72.41\,\%\), so this is substantially more demanding than
electron counting. 
A contributor-set error can still occur when the predicted dominant origin and the event electron count remain correct. The model performs the worst in predicting mixed hits with labels $1 + 3$, which has a geometric interpretation as these showers are in mean the furthest away, according to the canonical ordering.

\subsubsection{Electron Counting and the Contribution of Trigger-Pad Information}

Electron counting is the strongest result of the contributor-set model. The
derived count is correct for \(99.983\,\%\) of the \(100{,}000\) test events
%: \(49{,}931\) of \(49{,}934\) true \(2e\) events and \(50{,}052\) of \(50{,}066\) true \(3e\) events. As shown in
. Figure~\ref{fig:results_count_confusion}, errors occur in both directions but
are rare. This result should be interpreted within the restricted
scope of the experiment. The model is evaluated only on \(2e\) and \(3e\)
events and it should be recognzied that this is a simpler task. The model essentially solves a binary multiplicity distinction through its three
slot-validity predictions and it has not been trained to count an unrestricted range of incident electrons, as compared to the \gls{tpad} for example.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{config/fig/results/contributor_count_neural_1m.png}
    \caption{Electron count derived from the contributor model's predicted
    slot-validity mask on the \(100{,}000\)-event test set. The model receives
    both ECal and \gls{tpad} inputs. Rows are normalized within the true
    electron count; cell annotations show the event count and row percentage.}
    \label{fig:results_count_confusion}

The raw \gls{tpad} multiplicity in
Figure~\ref{fig:results_tpad_multiplicity} provides detector context rather
than a competing trained classifier. Its disagreement with the true electron count is strongly asymmetric and almost every mismatch is an undercount,
while there are som spurious overcounts. This is consistent with the
conservative \gls{tpad} track construction, for which an incident electron
may fail to produce a separate reconstructed candidate when signals are
missing or merged. Direct token-count equality therefore reaches \(79.9\,\%\)
over the balanced \(2e/3e\) sample, but it is not equivalent to the neural
task. The neural model may combine the ECal shower pattern with \gls{tpad}
positions, photoelectron measurements and multiplicity, and it only has to
distinguish \(2e\) from \(3e\). Its much higher accuracy should therefore
not be read as an apples-to-apples improvement over a stand-alone \gls{tpad} counting algorithm, but it does show promise to explore the electron counting assignment further in future work.

The contribution of \gls{tpad} information is instead tested by removing its
tokens from the same trained network at inference time.
Table~\ref{tab:results_tpad_count} reports this paired companion study using
the earlier \(100{,}000\)-event development model and its \(10{,}000\)-event
test set.

\input{config/tables/results_tpad_count}

The paired ablation reduces overall count accuracy by \(1.64\) percentage
points, with a \(95\,\%\) event-bootstrap interval of
\([1.39,1.89]\) percentage points. Almost the entire effect occurs in true
\(3e\) events, for which removing \gls{tpad} information reduces accuracy by
\(3.25\) percentage points. The interval excludes zero, demonstrating
statistically resolved sensitivity to removing the \gls{tpad} input for the
variable-count task.

Together with the fixed-count ablation in
Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_tpad_ablation}, these results show
task-dependent sensitivity to \gls{tpad}-token removal: the measured change
is small for baseline hit assignment when multiplicity is given, but larger
when the contributor model must infer multiplicity. Both are inference-time
ablations and therefore introduce a distribution shift; neither alone
isolates the informational value of the removed detector modality. A
separately trained ECal-only contributor model is needed to complete the
controlled architecture comparison and is treated as follow-up work rather
than folded into the current estimate.
