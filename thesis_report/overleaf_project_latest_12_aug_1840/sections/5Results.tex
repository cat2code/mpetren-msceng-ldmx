This chapter first characterises the simulated overlay events and the
geometric shower-overlap problem. It then evaluates Transformer and GravNet
baselines with fixed electron multiplicity before turning to the additional
counting, contributor-set and energy-fraction tasks of the experimental model
inspired by particle-flow reconstruction. Results and their immediate
interpretation are presented together.

\subsection{Event Characteristics and the Shower-Overlap Problem}
\label{sec:results_event_characteristics}

Before comparing reconstruction models, this subsection establishes the
detector, longitudinal and transverse scales of the problem that they must
solve. Unless stated otherwise, the descriptive figures use deterministic
cache prefixes containing \(1{,}000{,}000\) \(2e\) events and
\(1{,}000{,}000\) \(3e\)
events. These distributions characterise the simulated samples; they are not
used to select a model or tune a decision threshold.

\subsubsection{Detector Occupancy and Longitudinal Shower Development}

Figure~\ref{fig:results_shower_xy_context} shows the pooled transverse
distribution of reconstructed \gls{ecal} hits in detector coordinates. The
highest occupancy and energy density occur around the beam region, although
hits populate a much larger fraction of the instrumented area. The discrete
pattern of populated bins follows the hexagonal cell geometry and should not
be interpreted as a continuous image. Increasing the multiplicity from
\(2e\) to \(3e\) raises both the occupancy and the summed energy density while
preserving the detector-scale structure. On average, the selected \(2e\)
events contain \(247.2\) reconstructed hits and \(16.01\,\mathrm{GeV}\) of
reconstructed energy, compared with \(354.6\) hits and
\(24.02\,\mathrm{GeV}\) for \(3e\).

The same showers are resolved in depth in
Figure~\ref{fig:results_shower_longitudinal}. For both multiplicities, the
mean hit count is largest at \gls{ecal} layer~9, whereas the mean reconstructed
energy reaches its maximum at layer~8. These positions correspond to
\(z\simeq362\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(z\simeq343\,\mathrm{mm}\), respectively.
Both quantities subsequently decrease through a long tail in the calorimeter.
The \(3e\) profiles have larger amplitudes but nearly the same longitudinal
shape as the \(2e\) profiles. Adding an electron therefore primarily increases
the number and energy of simultaneous deposits rather than shifting the
characteristic shower maximum. The sizeable event-to-event RMS values near
the peak also demonstrate that the mean profile is not representative of
every individual event.

\subsubsection{Truth-Level Transverse Shower Geometry}

The truth-assisted geometry is defined in
Equations~\eqref{eq:shower_geometry_weight}--\eqref{eq:min_shower_separation}.
It weights each hit by reconstructed energy and the simulated contribution
fraction, so a mixed hit can contribute to several shower geometries. These
quantities are unavailable in measured detector data and are used only to
characterise the sample and interpret model performance.

Figure~\ref{fig:results_truth_centroid_separation} reports the closest
pair of resulting shower centroids in each event, resolved separately along
the two transverse coordinates.
The effective Moli\`ere radius \(R_{\mathrm{M}}=25\,\mathrm{mm}\) provides the
reference scale. For \(2e\) events, the median minimum separation is
\(0.253R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(6.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)) in \(x\) and
\(0.946R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(23.7\,\mathrm{mm}\)) in \(y\). The corresponding
\(3e\) medians are \(0.093R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(2.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)) and
\(0.334R_{\mathrm{M}}\) (\(8.3\,\mathrm{mm}\)). The difference between the
\(x\) and \(y\)
distributions is a property of the generated sample rather than a universal
detector-resolution limit. Moreover, a \(3e\) event contains three distinct
electron pairs instead of one. Taking the closest pair therefore shifts the
minimum-separation distribution towards smaller values even if the
distribution for a single pair were unchanged.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{config/fig/results/shower_centroid_separation.png}
    \caption{Minimum pairwise separation of energy-fraction-weighted truth
    shower centroids along \(x\) (left) and \(y\) (right). The lower axes use
    units of \(R_{\mathrm{M}}=25\,\mathrm{mm}\), while the upper axes show the
    corresponding physical distance. Points and vertical bars show bin
    fractions and their Poisson RMS counting uncertainties. Values beyond the
    plotted range are collected in the final bin. Each distribution contains
    \(1{,}000{,}000\) events.}
    \label{fig:results_truth_centroid_separation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:results_truth_shower_widths} separates the scale of an
individual shower from the distance between showers. The median transverse
widths are almost independent of event multiplicity: for both samples,
\(\sigma_x\simeq0.67R_{\mathrm{M}}\),
\(\sigma_y\simeq0.65R_{\mathrm{M}}\) and
\(\sigma_r\simeq0.96R_{\mathrm{M}}\). In contrast, the median minimum
width-normalised separation from
Equation~\eqref{eq:normalised_shower_separation} decreases from \(0.724\) for
\(2e\) to \(0.353\) for \(3e\). The individual
showers therefore have similar transverse dimensions in the two samples, but
the closest competing showers occupy a substantially more overlapping regime
when a third electron is present. This provides a physical explanation for
expecting the \(3e\) hit-assignment task to be more difficult.

\subsubsection{Trigger-Pad Multiplicity as Event-Level Context}
\begin{wrapfigure}[30]{r}{0.44\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{config/fig/results/shower_tpad_token_multiplicity.png}
    \caption{Confusion matrix with observed \gls{tpad}-track token multiplicity vs true
    electron multiplicity. Each column contains \(1{,}000{,}000\)
    events. Red outlines show agreement
    between the token and generated counts.}
    \label{fig:results_tpad_multiplicity}
\end{wrapfigure}
The \gls{tpad} tracks provide a small set of upstream position and
photoelectron measurements in addition to the \gls{ecal} hits. Their token
multiplicity is already an informative 
electron-count proxy, but it is not a
complete one. Figure~\ref{fig:results_tpad_multiplicity} shows that the
observed multiplicity equals the generated count in \(85.7\,\%\) of \(2e\)
events and \(74.0\,\%\) of \(3e\) events. Most of the remaining \(2e\) events
contain one token (\(13.9\,\%\)), while \(23.7\,\%\) of the \(3e\) events
contain only two. A further \(2.2\,\%\) of the \(3e\) sample contains only one
token, whereas overcounting is negligible for both multiplicities. Across the
balanced \(2e/3e\) sample, direct equality gives \(79.9\,\%\)
electron-count accuracy. The multiplicity therefore carries useful global
information, but missing, merged or otherwise unresolved tracks prevent it
from determining the electron count by itself. This motivates testing whether
a model can combine the \gls{tpad} observations with the \gls{ecal} shower
pattern.

\clearpage

In summary, the event statistics establish the expectations used to
interpret the model results. The individual shower widths change little from
\(2e\) to \(3e\), whereas the closest-pair separation decreases
substantially for \(3e\); the additional difficulty should therefore
arise mainly from overlap rather than broader individual showers. The
\gls{tpad} multiplicity supplies useful but incomplete event-level context.

\subsection{Baseline Models}

\subsubsection{Baseline Architecture and Performance Comparison}

The headline baseline comparison uses \(1{,}000{,}000\) selected events for
each electron multiplicity: \(800{,}000\) training, \(100{,}000\) validation
and \(100{,}000\) test events.
Both architectures receive the same log-transformed ECal and trigger-pad-track
features and use a hidden width of 128. The Transformer contains three
pre-layer-normalised self-attention layers with four attention heads and a
final layer normalisation. The GravNet model contains four neighbourhood
aggregation blocks, each using \(k=16\) neighbours, with batch normalisation
between blocks. Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_architecture} summarises the
resulting model sizes and observed training times.

\input{config/tables/results_baseline_architecture}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:method_capacity_selection}, these models were
selected as performance-oriented reference configurations rather than through
an exhaustive capacity search. The comparison below therefore evaluates the
implemented architectures, not an optimal accuracy--parameter or
accuracy--latency frontier. In particular, the use of contemporary \gls{mlpf}
models as a qualitative scale reference does not imply matched capacity: the
CMS particle-flow problem has different inputs, outputs and reconstruction
scope. No pruning or other compression study was performed.

The GravNet baseline has approximately \(36\,\%\) fewer trainable parameters
and correspondingly smaller model weights and checkpoints. In these runs,
however, this did not translate into shorter epochs: Transformer optimisation
took approximately 38--40 minutes per epoch, compared with 47--50 minutes for
GravNet. The learned neighbour search and aggregation are more expensive than
the parameter count alone suggests. This timing comparison remains
descriptive rather than controlled because the Transformer used a batch size
of 64 and GravNet used 32. As discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:method_capacity_selection}, these are performance-oriented
reference configurations rather than a capacity-optimised comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_performance} shows that both architectures
reach very similar shower-assignment performance. The \(3e\) task is
consistently harder than the \(2e\) task, independent of architecture. Energy
weighting increases all four accuracies, which indicates that misassigned
hits tend to carry less energy than correctly assigned hits. These headline
trainer summaries retain the canonical labels; the permutation-aligned
diagnostics begin in Section~\ref{sec:results_detailed_hit_assignment}.

\input{config/tables/results_baseline_performance}

Because both models were evaluated on the same validation events, their
event-wise results can be compared directly. Table~\ref{tab:results_baseline_paired}
reports paired bootstrap intervals for this comparison. The Transformer has a
\(0.223\)-percentage-point advantage in mean \(2e\) event accuracy and a
\(0.445\)-percentage-point advantage for \(3e\). The corresponding
energy-weighted advantages are \(0.203\) and \(0.510\) percentage points. All
four intervals exclude zero. The large event samples therefore resolve a
consistent Transformer advantage for these trained models, but its magnitude
remains approximately half a percentage point or less. Moreover, the
confidence intervals describe finite-event uncertainty for fixed trained
models; they do not include variation between random initialisations or
training samples. The results support similar practical performance with a
small advantage to the Transformer in this campaign, not a universal ranking
of the two architectures.

\input{config/tables/results_baseline_paired}

Since the two baselines behave similarly and the Transformer gives the higher
headline accuracy, the following detailed diagnostic plots use it as the
representative fixed-multiplicity model instead of duplicating every plot for
GravNet. These diagnostics were produced from the earlier
\(20{,}000\)-event development runs. They are retained to examine where
assignment fails, while Tables~\ref{tab:results_baseline_performance}
and~\ref{tab:results_baseline_paired} provide the headline comparison of the
larger matched runs.

The native-label accuracy is already \(0.828\) for training and \(0.832\) for

the first recorded epoch and later epochs mainly adjusted the predicted class

is slightly lower than the training loss throughout. This is consistent with
dropout being active while training metrics are accumulated, but disabled for
validation, which is evaluated once after each epoch with fixed parameters.
Smaller \(10{,}000\)-event runs showed similar qualitative behaviour and were
therefore useful for development, while all headline comparisons use the

\subsection{MLPF-Inspired Slot Model}

\subsubsection{MLPF-Inspired Contributor-Set Reconstruction}

The experimental contributor-set slot model extends the fixed-multiplicity
assignment problem to a particle-flow-like multi-task setting. The final model
was trained on one million events balanced between the \(2e\) and \(3e\)
sources and evaluated on a separate \(100{,}000\)-event test set. Its
epoch-15 checkpoint is used for the headline results in
Table~\ref{tab:results_contributor_performance}. The backbone has hidden width
192, three pre-layer-normalised Transformer layers and eight attention heads,
corresponding to approximately \(1.16\) million trainable parameters.

The model predicts three electron-slot validity scores for each event and a
contributor-set distribution and energy fractions for each hit. The electron
count is derived from the valid slots; it is not produced by a separate count
head. Similarly, the dominant-origin label is derived from the predicted
fractions. This post-processing keeps the reported quantities consistent with
the model's underlying set-valued prediction.

\input{config/tables/results_contributor_performance}

The detailed diagnostic figures below were produced with the preceding
development model, trained on \(50{,}000\) \(2e\) and \(50{,}000\) \(3e\)
events. Its best checkpoint was selected at epoch 14 and evaluated on
\(10{,}000\) events containing \(3{,}025{,}349\) ECal hits. The resulting
metrics are close to those of the final model and are used here to resolve the
same qualitative error patterns.

Figure~\ref{fig:results_contributor_set_confusion} resolves the exact
contributor-set task. Pure one-electron contributions dominate the data and
are generally reconstructed more reliably than mixed sets. The overall exact
set accuracy is \(72.39\,\%\), so this is substantially more demanding than
electron counting. In particular, a contributor-set error can occur even when
the predicted dominant origin and the event electron count remain correct.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

Figure~\ref{fig:results_contributor_hit_tasks} shows two useful reductions of
the same predictions. Dominant-origin accuracy reaches \(77.09\,\%\), which is
comparable in scale to the fixed-multiplicity \(3e\) baseline despite the
additional event-count and fraction tasks. Mixed-hit detection reaches
\(64.69\,\%\) recall but only \(46.49\,\%\) precision, giving an \(F_1\) score
of \(0.541\). The model therefore identifies a majority of truth-mixed hits but
also labels a sizeable number of pure hits as mixed. This task should not be
summarised by ordinary accuracy alone because pure hits are much more common.

Applying the predicted slot-validity mask before normalising the energy
fractions reduces their mean absolute error from \(0.144\) to \(0.115\). This
improvement illustrates one purpose of coupling the tasks: the event-level
validity prediction constrains which electron fractions are physically
available during post-processing. Nevertheless, the contributor-set and
mixed-hit results show that resolving overlap at hit level remains the main
weakness of this preliminary model.

\subsubsection{Electron Counting and the Contribution of Trigger-Pad Information}

Electron counting is the strongest result of the contributor-set model. The
final model derives the correct count for \(99.983\,\%\) of its test events,
with similarly high performance for true \(2e\) and \(3e\) samples. To test
whether trigger-pad information contributes to this task,
Table~\ref{tab:results_tpad_count} uses the companion development model and
its \(10{,}000\)-event test set. It compares the full model with both an
inference-time removal of the trigger-pad tokens and a direct heuristic that
equates observed trigger-pad multiplicity with electron count. The full
development model attains \(99.79\,\%\) accuracy.

The paired ablation reduces overall count accuracy by \(1.64\) percentage
points, with a \(95\,\%\) event-bootstrap interval of
\([1.39,1.89]\) percentage points. Almost the entire effect occurs in true
\(3e\) events, for which removing trigger-pad information reduces accuracy by
\(3.25\) percentage points. The interval excludes zero, providing evidence
that this trained network uses the trigger-pad input for the variable-count
task.

Together with the fixed-count ablation in
Figure~\ref{fig:results_tpad_ablation}, these results show a task-dependent
role for trigger-pad information: it has negligible measured effect on the
baseline's hit assignment when multiplicity is given, but a clear effect when
the contributor model must infer multiplicity. The present ablation still
introduces a distribution shift because trigger-pad inputs are removed only at
inference time. A separately trained ECal-only contributor model is needed to
complete the controlled architecture comparison and is treated as follow-up
work rather than folded into the current estimate.
